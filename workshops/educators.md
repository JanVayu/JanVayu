# Air literacy for educators

One hour, written for teachers of Class 9 and above. You will leave with a
lesson you can run on Monday, an assessment you do not have to write, and a
correct understanding of the one thing most air-quality teaching gets wrong.

No science background assumed. Everything used here is free and needs only a
browser.

> A note for the facilitator: on Workshopy's free plan a session runs 45
> minutes. Steps 1 to 6 fit inside that. Step 7 onwards can be a second session.

# Why this belongs in a classroom

Not as a general environmental topic. As something that is happening to the
students in front of you.

India's national annual average PM2.5 is **48.9 µg/m³** (IQAir 2025), against a
WHO guideline of **5** and India's own legal limit of **40**. The average Indian
loses about **3.5 years** of life expectancy to it (AQLI 2025), and outdoor
PM2.5 is associated with about **1.72 million** deaths a year (Lancet Countdown
2025).

Children are not simply small adults here. They breathe more air per kilogram of
body weight, their lungs are still developing, and they are shorter, which puts
them nearer the exhaust. Schools in Delhi-NCR close for it.

There is also a good pedagogical reason. Air quality is one of the few topics
where a student can pull a real, current, local measurement off a public
network in thirty seconds and then argue about what it means. That is what
science looks like, and it is hard to arrange in most syllabus topics.

# The one thing to get right: PM2.5, not AQI

If a student leaves your class with one idea, make it this one.

**AQI is an index.** It is built from up to six pollutants and it reports only
whichever one scores worst. Two cities both reading 200 can be breathing very
different air. It is also scaled differently in India and the United States, so
the same air gets two different AQI numbers.

**PM2.5 is a measurement**, in micrograms per cubic metre. Every Indian legal
limit, every health study and every policy target is written in it.

The physical reason PM2.5 matters is teachable in one minute and worth the
minute. PM10 is about 10 micrometres across; the nose and throat catch most of
it and the cilia sweep much of it back out. PM2.5 is 2.5 micrometres or smaller,
small enough to reach the alveoli and cross into the bloodstream. A human hair is
about 70 micrometres, roughly 28 times wider than a PM2.5 particle.

That is the whole mechanism, and it explains why this is not only a lung
problem: PM2.5 is linked to heart attack and stroke, kidney decline, cognitive
loss, and low birth weight.

# Make it tangible

Abstract micrograms do not land. Three devices that do.

**The cigarette equivalence.** Berkeley Earth's rule of thumb: one cigarette is
roughly 22 µg/m³ of PM2.5 over a day. So a day at 110 µg/m³ is about five
cigarettes. Teach it honestly: this is an illustration of inhaled particulate
dose, not a medical equivalence, and saying so is part of the lesson.

**The hair.** Hold up a hair. PM2.5 is a thirtieth of that width.

**Their own ward.** A number for their own neighbourhood beats a national
average every time. See step 6.

# The games

Seven browser games, written with Indian content rather than translated from
American material. All free, no login, and each runs in a single lesson or less.

**Air Quality Jeopardy, India edition.** A board game for teams. The longest of
the seven and the best for a full period. Modelled on the classroom Jeopardy
that Dr Sarath Guttikunda has run live for years at UrbanEmissions.info.

**PM Quick-Quiz.** Short, individual, good as a warm-up or an exit ticket.

**Source Matcher.** Match a pollution source to what it emits. This is the one
that fixes the common misconception that all air pollution is vehicles.

**Clean Air Snakes and Ladders.** After *Moksha Patam*, the original Indian
game. Good for younger groups and for mixed-ability classes.

**Jodi Match.** A memory-card game on pollutant pairs.

**Air Tambola.** Housie, with air-quality content. Works with a large group and
almost no setup.

**Vayu Junction.** A word-grouping puzzle in the *Only Connect* style. The
hardest of the seven, and the best for a strong senior group.

> **Do this now.** Play one of them for five minutes and decide which fits the
> class you are actually teaching next week.

# A ward-map exercise that works

This is the lesson to run if you only run one.

Open **[janvayu.in/#map](https://www.janvayu.in/#map)**. It covers 983,149
administrative areas, including all 584,615 villages and 68,596 municipal wards,
each with an annual PM2.5 figure.

**The task.** Each student finds their own ward or village, writes down its
annual PM2.5, and compares it with India's limit of 40 and the WHO guideline of
5. Then they find the cleanest and dirtiest ward in the same city.

**The discussion.** Why is there a spread within one city at all? What is
different about those two places? Is it traffic, industry, tree cover, or how
the map's one-kilometre grid happens to fall?

**The teachable correction.** Students will reach for "green cover" as the
explanation. Show them that green cover counts cropland, so a typical Indian
district is about 97% green and about 14% treed, and that the honest measure for
this question is **tree cover**. We made that error ourselves before correcting
it, which is a useful thing to tell a class.

**The discipline to insist on.** The map shows live dots and annual shading at
the same time. They are not two versions of one number. Today's reading answers
"should I run this evening"; the annual figure answers "what is it like to grow
up here". A student who can keep those apart has understood something most
newspaper coverage has not.

# The self-check, and using it as assessment

The **Air-Literacy Quiz** on JanVayu is a 10-question self-check on the
essentials: what AQI actually measures, how India's standards compare with the
WHO's, and what to do on a bad day.

Every answer carries an explanation, so it teaches while it tests. That makes it
usable in three ways:

- **As a pre-test**, to find out what the class already believes
- **As an exit ticket** at the end of a lesson
- **As a self-paced revision tool** students can repeat

Pair it with the ward exercise in step 6 and you have a full assessment: a
factual component and an applied one, neither of which you had to write.
