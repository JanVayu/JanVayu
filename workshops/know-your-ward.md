# Know your ward

Thirty minutes. By the end you will know your own neighbourhood's air, how hot its
ground runs, how much of it is under tree canopy, and the one distinction that
stops people misreading all three.

You need a phone or a laptop and a browser. Nothing to install.

Open **[janvayu.in/#map](https://www.janvayu.in/#map)** and leave it open beside this.

# Find yourself on the map

India is divided into 983,149 administrative areas and every one of them is on
this map: 36 states, 785 districts, 6,471 blocks and tehsils, 319,287 gram
panchayats, 584,615 villages, 3,359 city bodies and 68,596 municipal wards.

Use the **Boundaries** menu to choose the level you live at. If you are in a
town or a city, that is **City or ULB, then Ward**. If you are not, it is
**District, then Block, then Gram panchayat, then Village**.

You do not need to know in advance which of those you are. Pick either and zoom
in until you find the name you recognise.

> **Do this now.** Find the boundary you live inside and tap it. Write down the
> annual PM2.5 figure it gives you.

Mark this step done once you have your own number.

# The one thing people get wrong

Look at the map and you will see two different things at once.

The **dots** are live readings from roughly 565 continuous CPCB monitors,
updated through the day. They tell you about this hour.

The **shading underneath** is an annual average for 2024, estimated from
satellite at about one kilometre. It tells you about a whole year.

These are not two versions of the same number and they must never be put in one
sentence. A live reading of 180 on a November morning and an annual average of
72 are both true and they answer different questions.

The practical rule: **use the live dot to decide about today, use the shading to
decide about where you live.** Masks, school runs and exercise belong to the
dot. Moving house, planting trees, and asking your corporator for a monitor
belong to the shading.

One more limit worth stating. At one kilometre the satellite layer smooths over
small sources. A brick kiln, a crusher or a highway immediately next door will
not appear as its own hotspot.

# Heat and trees, on the same map

Switch the **Colour** menu from air to **surface heat**, then to **tree cover**,
then to **built-up share**. The same boundaries, shaded by something else.

Two cautions, both of which have caught us.

**Surface heat is ground temperature, not air temperature.** It is what a
Landsat pass measures coming off roofs, roads and soil under a clear
pre-monsoon sky. It runs well above what a weather forecast will tell you, so
do not read it as "it will be 47 degrees on Tuesday".

**Green cover is not trees.** The green measure counts cropland, shrub and grass
along with canopy, so the typical Indian district comes out about 97% green and
about 14% treed. If you want to know about trees, use the **tree cover** layer.
We got this wrong ourselves first: green cover barely moved with heat at all
(a correlation of −0.07 nationally), and tree cover did.

> **Do this now.** Find the most treed and least treed ward in your city and
> compare their surface heat. Then compare their annual air.

# What you can do with this

You now have a number that applies to where you actually live, which for most of
India has never existed before. Roughly 565 continuous monitors serve 584,615
villages, so the live network structurally cannot answer "how is my village's
air", and never will.

Three things this is good for.

**Asking for a monitor.** If your ward is above India's annual limit of 40 and
has no station within a useful distance, that is a concrete thing to put in
writing to your municipal corporation.

**Arguing about trees.** Tree cover and surface heat are on the same map at the
same boundary. A ward with single-digit canopy and high surface heat is an
argument you can show someone.

**Filing an RTI.** JanVayu's RTI Assistant will pre-fill a Right to Information
request about your city's monitoring network or its clean-air spending. It costs
₹10, it is free if you hold a BPL card, and the reply is due in 30 days.

If you want the longer version of any of this, the blog post
[How to Read the JanVayu Map](https://www.janvayu.in/blog/#/posts/2026-08-09-how-to-read-the-map)
is the manual we should have written first.
