# Project background

## Origin

JanVayu began as a civic response to the repeated failure of annual pollution "seasons" in North India to produce lasting policy accountability. Every winter, Delhi's air quality collapses. Every spring, the crisis recedes from public memory. The cycle repeats.

The project rests on one conviction: public accountability needs public memory. If the data, the court orders, the policy failures and the human stories are archived and kept permanently accessible, they cannot easily be dismissed or forgotten.

---

## Name

**JanVayu** (जनवायु) translates as "People's Air". It joins *jan* (जन, people or public) and *vayu* (वायु, air or wind). The name was chosen in January 2026 after community input, and replaced the earlier name "Vayu Smriti" (वायु स्मृति, Air Memory). It was picked because it works better across India's diverse language communities.

In Tamil: ஜன்வாயு | In Bengali: জনবায়ু | In Marathi: जनवायु

---

## Scope

JanVayu covers the whole country: 157 Indian cities across all major regions, and not only Delhi-NCR, with Beijing, London and Singapore as international comparators. It also looks at who is exposed unequally:

- Women, who are disproportionately exposed to household air pollution
- Outdoor workers, who spend long hours in the open air ([Barthwal et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9200945/), outdoor workers in Delhi)
- Scheduled-caste and scheduled-tribe populations, who are more likely to be exposed to pollution from coal-fired plants ([Kopas et al. 2020, *Ecological Economics* 176](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolec/v176y2020ics0921800919304197.html))
- Children, whose developing lungs are the most vulnerable

## Governance

JanVayu is non-partisan: air quality affects every Indian regardless of political affiliation, and the data, analysis and accountability work serve everyone. It is maintained independently, so decisions follow editorial principles and not donor requirements. The code is MIT-licensed, and the content is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

Editorial principles:
1. Factual accuracy and source transparency
2. Respect for affected communities
3. Criticism of policies and systems, not of parties or individuals
4. Accessibility across languages and literacy levels
