Understanding AQI

The Air Quality Index is a composite number — it takes readings from multiple pollutants and reports the worst one. Most people fixate on PM2.5, but over-focusing on a single pollutant means overlooking other harmful gases that may be driving the AQI in your city. This guide breaks down what AQI actually measures, what each pollutant does to your body, and why the raw numbers matter more than the label.

What is AQI?

AQI is a composite index. Monitoring stations measure up to 8 pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, SO2, O3, CO, NH3, Pb). Each pollutant concentration is converted into a sub-index on a 0–500 scale using breakpoint tables. The final AQI = maximum of all sub-indices. The pollutant with the highest sub-index is called the dominant pollutant.

Why this matters

An AQI of 200 could be driven by PM2.5 alone OR by NO2 alone — the health response should differ. PM2.5-driven AQI calls for N95 masks and air purifiers. NO2-driven AQI calls for avoiding traffic corridors and idling vehicles. Same number, different action.

CPCB also includes NH3 and Pb in its sub-index calculation, but these are measured at fewer stations. The six pollutants above are the most commonly reported.

The Six Criteria Pollutants

These are the primary pollutants that determine your city’s AQI. Each has distinct sources, health effects, and regulatory limits. Tap any card to learn more.

PM2.5 — Fine Particulate Matter (<2.5µm)

What: Tiny particles less than 2.5 micrometres in diameter — small enough to penetrate deep into lungs and cross into the bloodstream.

Sources: Vehicles, cooking with solid fuels, crop burning, construction dust, industrial emissions, power plants.

Why it matters: The #1 air pollution killer — responsible for an estimated 1.72 million Indian deaths per year (Lancet Countdown 2025). Causes heart disease, stroke, lung cancer, COPD, and reduced life expectancy.

NAAQS: 40 µg/m³ annual WHO: 5 µg/m³ annual
PM10 — Coarse Particulate Matter (<10µm)

What: Larger particles — dust, pollen, mould spores, construction debris — that settle in the upper airways.

Sources: Road dust, construction sites, mining, agricultural tilling, desert storms.

Why it matters: Causes respiratory inflammation, aggravates asthma and bronchitis. Often dominates AQI in dust-prone cities across Rajasthan, Gujarat, and western UP — but gets less media attention than PM2.5.

NAAQS: 60 µg/m³ annual WHO: 15 µg/m³ annual
NO2 — Nitrogen Dioxide

What: A reddish-brown gas produced by high-temperature combustion. Gives smoggy air its brownish tint.

Sources: Vehicle exhaust (especially diesel), power plants, industrial boilers.

Why it matters: Causes airway inflammation, increases frequency and severity of asthma attacks. A key precursor to ground-level ozone. NO2 is rising rapidly in Indian metro cities due to growing diesel vehicle fleets — a silent crisis.

NAAQS: 40 µg/m³ annual WHO: 10 µg/m³ annual
SO2 — Sulfur Dioxide

What: A pungent, irritating gas produced when sulfur-containing fuels are burned.

Sources: Coal-fired power plants, industrial processes (smelting, refining), oil refineries, diesel vehicles.

Why it matters: Causes acid rain, respiratory irritation, bronchoconstriction, and worsens cardiovascular disease. Even short-term spikes can trigger hospital admissions in people with asthma or heart conditions.

NAAQS: 50 µg/m³ annual WHO: 40 µg/m³ (24hr)
O3 — Ground-level Ozone

What: NOT the protective ozone layer — this is toxic ozone at ground level, formed by photochemical reactions.

Sources: Formed when NOx + VOCs react in sunlight. Not emitted directly. Worst on hot, sunny afternoons — peaks in summer, not winter.

Why it matters: Triggers asthma attacks, reduces lung function, damages agricultural crops. Rising in Indian cities but barely discussed because India’s pollution narrative focuses on winter PM2.5. O3 is the pollutant most people have never heard of.

NAAQS: 100 µg/m³ (8hr) WHO: 60 µg/m³ (peak season)
CO — Carbon Monoxide

What: An odourless, colourless gas produced by incomplete combustion. Undetectable without instruments.

Sources: Vehicle exhaust, biomass burning (chulha, crop residue), industrial processes, charcoal/coal heating (angithi).

Why it matters: Binds to haemoglobin 200× more strongly than oxygen, reducing the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity. Fatal in enclosed spaces — every winter, families die from angithi CO poisoning in northern India.

NAAQS: 2 mg/m³ (8hr) WHO: 4 mg/m³ (24hr)
Why PM2.5 Isn’t the Whole Story

India’s air pollution conversation is dominated by PM2.5 — understandably, since it is the deadliest. But focusing exclusively on one pollutant creates dangerous blind spots:

Different AQI, different response

A city with AQI 150 from PM2.5 vs AQI 150 from O3 requires completely different responses. PM2.5 calls for masks and purifiers; O3 calls for staying indoors during peak sunlight hours. Masks do not filter ozone.

PM10: the forgotten pollutant

PM10 dominates AQI in Rajasthan, Gujarat, and western India due to desert dust and road dust resuspension — but gets a fraction of the media coverage PM2.5 receives. Dust-driven AQI crises are real health events.

NO2: the silent crisis

NO2 is rising rapidly in metro cities due to growing diesel vehicle fleets. It is a direct health hazard and a precursor to ozone formation. The dual threat makes NO2 reduction one of the highest-impact interventions.

O3: surging in summer

Ozone is the pollutant most people have never heard of but is surging in Indian summers. It peaks precisely when PM2.5 drops — creating a false sense of “clean air” in months when ozone may be dangerously high.

India’s NAAQS vs WHO guidelines

India’s National Ambient Air Quality Standard for PM2.5 is 40 µg/m³ (annual average). The WHO 2021 guideline is 5 µg/m³ — an 8× gap. What CPCB labels “Moderate” would be classified “Unhealthy” by global standards. Always look at raw µg/m³ values, not just the AQI label.

CPCB vs US EPA AQI Scales

India’s CPCB and the US EPA use different breakpoint tables to convert the same PM2.5 concentration into an AQI number. The CPCB scale is significantly more lenient — masking the true severity of exposure.

Key insight

At 100 µg/m³ PM2.5, CPCB still says “Moderate” while the US EPA says “Unhealthy.” CPCB’s “Moderate” category hides what the rest of the world classifies as dangerous air. Always look at the raw µg/m³ concentration, not just the AQI number or colour code.
