CPCB CAAQMS
Government regulatory Hourly ±5-10% accuracy

Instruments: BAM/TEOM (beta attenuation / tapered element oscillating microbalance) — reference-grade instruments used worldwide for regulatory monitoring.

Coverage: ~586 continuous real-time (CAAQMS) stations in CPCB’s national network (aqicn.org CPCB network count, 2026).

STRENGTHS

Official, legally admissible, calibrated instruments with traceable standards.

WEAKNESSES

Sparse coverage (2-3 stations per non-NCR city). 88% of stations flouted CPCB siting criteria (Newslaundry investigation, 2025); a 2025 CAG audit found 13 of 24 Delhi stations too close to trees or buildings.

WAQI / aqicn.org
Aggregator Hourly Depends on upstream

Instruments: Surfaces CPCB data + community sensors. Accuracy depends entirely on the upstream source.

Coverage: Global — 50,000+ stations across ~2,000 cities in 132 countries (aqicn.org, 2026).

STRENGTHS

Single API for global comparison. JanVayu’s primary data source for live AQI.

WEAKNESSES

Returns nearest station only (not city average). May lag slightly behind the underlying CPCB feed.

IQAir
Commercial aggregator Hourly (live) / Annual (report) Blended methodology

Instruments: CPCB + proprietary commercial sensors + satellite data. Blended methodology combining multiple inputs.

Coverage: 9,446 cities across 143 countries (IQAir 2025 World Air Quality Report).

STRENGTHS

Widely cited in international press. Annual country/city rankings drive policy attention.

WEAKNESSES

Methodology not fully transparent. Commercial interest (sells air purifiers and monitors).

Sensor.Community
Citizen science (CC0 open data) Every 2.5 minutes ±20-50% vs regulatory

Instruments: SDS011/SPS30 laser scattering sensors (~$25-40 / ~₹2,000-3,300 each). Low-cost but widely deployed.

Coverage: a small footprint in India — part of a global network of ~12,000 active sensors, concentrated in Europe (sensor.community, 2026). Hyperlocal density where volunteers deploy them.

STRENGTHS

Hyperlocal density, fully open data (CC0), community-deployed. Fills gaps where government monitors don’t exist.

WEAKNESSES

Lower accuracy (±20-50%), calibration drift over time, sensor placement varies (balcony vs roadside vs rooftop).

Indian Open Maps — map geometry, not AQI
Community-run mirror Government geodata 574 datasets

What we use it for: ward boundaries beyond our original 15 cities (Swachh Bharat Mission), Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha constituency boundaries and districts (LGD/Bharatmaps), plus pollution-source and who-breathes-it overlays — landfills, dumpsites, coal mines, CPCB red/orange-category industrial parks, SEZs, schools (UDISE) and health centres.

Provenance, honestly: indianopenmaps.com is a volunteer project (ramSeraph) that scrapes and republishes geodata from official portals — SBM, Local Government Directory, Bharatmaps, GatiShakti, NCOG. Much of it is flagged “not-so-open” upstream: published by government, but without an explicit open licence. We ship simplified derivatives with attribution, and the coal-mine locations are CC0 (Harvard Dataverse). Boundary data is refreshed with scripts/fetch-openmaps.mjs, not live-scraped.

Caveat: ward boundaries are as ULBs uploaded them — a few cities publish coarse revenue wards (Jamnagar: 16; Rajkot, Vadodara: 18) or stale delimitations, and West Bengal, Assam, Manipur, Mizoram and Tripura are missing from the SBM ward set entirely — so no city in those states can be added from it. Kolkata is in the atlas from a separate hand-collected source, and Guwahati from a third one (below). If your ward looks wrong, that’s a data gap worth an RTI.

DataMeet, BharatLas & city portals — the other ward boundaries
CC BY ODbL-1.0 16 of 90 cities

What we use them for: the 16 Ward Atlas cities the Swachh Bharat release cannot supply. Thirteen — including Delhi, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad and Kolkata — come from DataMeet’s Municipal Spatial Data (CC BY). Guwahati comes from BharatLas, which republishes OpenCity / Oorvani Foundation layers under ODbL-1.0 — the 2022 GMC delimitation, and the only way to put a city with real winter pollution on the map when its whole state is absent from SBM. Mumbai and Varanasi come from the Mumbai spatial-data project and the Varanasi Smart City portal.

Attribution, honestly: these upstreams carry different licences with different obligations, so we don’t flatten them into one credit line. Every ward file records the source it actually came from, and the atlas prints that credit under the map for whichever city you are looking at. Boundaries are simplified derivatives (~28 m), built by scripts/import-bharatlas-wards.mjs and scripts/fetch-openmaps.mjs.

Satellite layers — annual air, heat, green & built-up
CC BY 4.0 Annual, not live 584,615 villages · 6,596 wards

What we use them for: the annual mean PM2.5 carried by every one of India’s 584,615 villages and all 6,596 wards comes from SatPM2.5 V6GL03 (ACAG, Washington University — a neural network over satellite aerosol depth combined with the GEOS-Chem model, ~1 km, CC BY 4.0, on AWS Open Data). The Ward Atlas’s heat layer is Landsat 8/9 surface temperature (~30 m) via Microsoft Planetary Computer; green cover and built-up are ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m, CC BY 4.0).

Two limits worth stating: this is an annual average, not today’s air — it says nothing about a bad week in November, which for the Indo-Gangetic plain is much of the story, so we never merge it with a live reading. And a ~1 km product smooths hyperlocal sources: a single kiln, smelter or busy junction next door will not show up in it. It is a modelled estimate calibrated against ground monitors, not a measurement taken in your village.

Why Source Transparency Matters

This is why source transparency matters. When a politician says “AQI improved 20%,” ask: which monitors? Which stations? Over what period? A 20% improvement measured by one station near a highway is meaningless if the station near the school was decommissioned.

Questions to always ask

  • Which source? — CPCB, WAQI, IQAir, or low-cost sensors?
  • How many stations? — City average from 15 stations, or one reading from one location?
  • What time window? — Hourly peak, 24-hour average, or annual mean?
  • Station changes? — Were monitors added, removed, or relocated?
  • Which AQI scale? — CPCB breakpoints are more lenient than US EPA breakpoints
