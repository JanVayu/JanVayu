Legal Framework for Clean Air

Constitutional rights, Supreme Court mandates, and statutory powers that provide the legal basis for emergency action on air pollution.

Constitutional & Judicial Basis

Article 21: Right to Life = Right to Clean Air

Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991): Supreme Court declared that the right to life encompasses the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air.

Right Against Climate Degradation (2024)

M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (2024): Fundamental right against climate degradation — frames air pollution as constitutional rights defense.

Polluter Pays Principle

Vellore Citizens v. UOI (1996): Integrated Precautionary and Polluter Pays Principles. Justifies pollution cess, fines on violators.

Absolute Liability

M.C. Mehta v. UOI (1987): Industries in hazardous activities are strictly liable regardless of negligence.

GRAP — Judicially Mandated Emergency Response

GRAP is a judicially approved mandate, approved by Supreme Court in 2016 in M.C. Mehta v. UOI (WP 13029/1985). CAQM implements it across NCR since 2021.

Your Legal Rights — At a Glance

Five pillars of law that protect your right to breathe clean air. Know them — cite them in your complaints.

Article 21 — Constitution of India

Right to life includes the right to clean air. MC Mehta v UoI (1986), Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar (1991).

Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Established CPCB and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). Mandates them to prevent, control, and abate air pollution. Section 31A: binding closure/direction orders.

Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Umbrella legislation. Central government can take measures for environmental protection, set emission standards, and order closure of polluting units. Sections 3 & 5.

National Green Tribunal Act, 2010

Created a dedicated tribunal for environmental disputes. Any person can file an application. Filing fee: ₹1,000 (waived for BPL). No lawyer required for individual applications.

Right to Information Act, 2005

Right to access government data on pollution monitoring, NCAP funds, enforcement actions. Free for BPL; ₹10 otherwise. 30-day response mandate. Use the RTI Assistant →

What Can I Do from Home? — Step-by-Step Citizen Recourse

You do not need to be a lawyer, activist, or expert. Here are five concrete steps any citizen can take from home to demand clean air. Each step is progressively more powerful.

STEP 1

File an RTI — Free, 30-day response guaranteed

The simplest and most powerful tool. Any citizen can demand pollution data, budget utilisation details, or enforcement records.

Who to write to:
  • CPCB PIO — for national monitoring data, emission norms, station uptime
  • Your State PCB PIO — for local station data, industry compliance, NCAP city plans
  • CAQM PIO — for GRAP enforcement records (NCR only)
What to ask:
  • CAAQMS data-availability percentage for your city, last 12 months
  • NCAP funds received vs. utilised by your city, FY 2023–2026
  • Number of industrial inspections and closure orders issued, last 1 year
  • GRAP enforcement: fines levied, vehicles impounded, sites shut (NCR)
STEP 2

File a Complaint with CPCB

Report specific violations — illegal burning, industrial emissions, construction dust — directly to CPCB online.

How to file:
  • Go to cpcb.nic.in → Grievance / Complaint section
  • Include: exact location, type of violation, date & time, photos/videos if possible
  • You can also file via the SAMEER app (CPCB's official app on Play Store)
  • Expected response time: 15–30 days

Tip: File with both CPCB and your State PCB simultaneously. It doubles the accountability.

STEP 3

Write to Your MP / MLA

Elected representatives respond to constituent pressure. A well-written letter citing local data is surprisingly effective.

Template letter (copy and customise):
Respected Shri/Smt [Name], I am a resident of [your locality], in [your constituency]. I am writing to draw your urgent attention to the air pollution crisis in our area. According to CPCB data, our city's annual average PM2.5 is [X] µg/m³ — that is [Y] times the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. This causes an estimated [Z] premature deaths annually in our district (Lancet Countdown 2025). I request you to: 1. Raise this issue in Parliament / State Assembly during the next session 2. Write to the Environment Minister seeking an NCAP review for our city 3. Ensure NCAP funds allocated to our city are fully utilised (as of March 2026, only [%] was spent) I am happy to share the full data report from JanVayu (janvayu.org) which sources all numbers from CPCB, NCAP, and peer-reviewed studies. Yours faithfully, [Your Name], [Your Address]

Find your representative: Election Commission of India  |  Lok Sabha Members Directory

STEP 4

Approach the National Green Tribunal (NGT)

The NGT is a dedicated environmental court. Any individual can file an application — no lawyer is needed for individual applications.

Key facts:
  • Filing fee: ₹1,000 for individuals (waived for BPL applicants)
  • Jurisdiction: Environmental damage, non-compliance with pollution norms, failure to implement court orders
  • Benches: Principal Bench (Delhi) + 4 zonal benches (Pune, Bhopal, Chennai, Kolkata)
  • Timeline: NGT is mandated to dispose cases within 6 months
  • Filing: Can file online at greentribunal.gov.in

Note: For complex cases or those involving multiple states/industries, engaging an environmental lawyer is recommended. Many NGOs offer pro-bono legal support.

STEP 5

File a PIL (Public Interest Litigation)

For systemic failures affecting a community. Filed in the High Court or Supreme Court. Any citizen can file — no special locus standi required.

How PIL works:
  • Who can file: Any citizen, on behalf of the public. No need to be personally affected.
  • Where: High Court of your state (Article 226) or Supreme Court (Article 32)
  • Requires: A lawyer, but many environmental lawyers work pro bono. Some HC registries accept a letter addressed to the Chief Justice.
  • Cost: Court fees vary by state; some HCs waive fees for PILs. SC filing fee is nominal (₹50).
  • Timeline: Can lead to interim orders within weeks if urgency is demonstrated
Landmark PILs that changed India's air:
  • MC Mehta v Union of India (1986): Led to CNG mandate in Delhi, vehicle-age limits, GRAP framework
  • Vellore Citizens v Union of India (1996): Established Polluter Pays and Precautionary Principles
  • Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar (1991): Right to clean air = Right to life under Article 21
  • M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India (2024): Right against climate degradation as a fundamental right
You have more power than you think. An RTI application costs ₹10 and 15 minutes. A complaint to CPCB costs nothing. A letter to your MP costs a stamp. Start with Step 1 today — the law is on your side.
