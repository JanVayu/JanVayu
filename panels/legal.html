Legal Framework for Clean Air

Constitutional rights, Supreme Court mandates, and statutory powers that provide the legal basis for emergency action on air pollution.

Constitutional & Judicial Basis

Article 21: Right to Life = Right to Clean Air

Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991): Supreme Court declared that the right to life encompasses the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air.

Right Against Climate Degradation (2024)

M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India (21 Mar 2024, 2024 INSC 280): The Court recognised a right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21. The case concerned Great Indian Bustard habitat and power lines, not air pollution.

Polluter Pays Principle

Vellore Citizens v. UOI (1996): Integrated Precautionary and Polluter Pays Principles. Justifies pollution cess, fines on violators.

Absolute Liability

M.C. Mehta v. UOI (1987): Industries in hazardous activities are strictly liable regardless of negligence.

MV Act, 1988 Section 190(2) Driving a vehicle that breaches air-pollution standards: up to 3 months' imprisonment or a fine up to ₹10,000, or both, plus licence disqualification (text as amended in 2019). Update (Oct 2026): the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2026 (Act 8 of 2026) substitutes s.190(1) and (2) of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988. For a vehicle that violates the standards prescribed in relation to road safety or control of air-pollution, the new s.190(2)(i) reads: "shall be liable for the first contravention with penalty which may extend to ten thousand rupees and he shall be disqualified for holding licence for a period of three months and for any second or subsequent offence with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months or with fine which may extend to ten thousand rupees, or with both". Under s.1(2) of the 2026 Act the Central Government appoints commencement dates by notification, and different dates may be appointed. A MoRTH notification (S.O. 4100(E)) appointing a commencement date for specified provisions relating to the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 has been reported, but the date and the provisions covered have not been confirmed (commencement date to be confirmed). Until then, treat the earlier text as the law in force unless the notification says otherwise. PUC norms are enforced separately from s.190(2): GRAP Stage I lists "Strict vigilance and enforcement of PUC norms for vehicles" (CAQM order of 13 December 2025).
SWM Rules, 2026 (superseding the SWM Rules, 2016) Multiple The Solid Waste Management Rules, 2026, notified under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, supersede the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 and "will come into full effect from April 1, 2026" (PIB, 28 Jan 2026); they provide for environmental compensation on the 'Polluter Pays' principle for non-compliance and improper solid waste management practices. Whether the 2026 text carries over an express open-burning prohibition has not been confirmed (to be confirmed against the Gazette text). Separately, the NGT directed the Delhi government on 12 Oct 2022 to pay ₹900 crore as environmental compensation for failing to remediate legacy waste at the Ghazipur, Bhalswa and Okhla landfills (ETV Bharat/PTI, 12 Oct 2022); that award concerns legacy landfill waste, not open burning.
GRAP: the court-mandated emergency response

GRAP is a judicially approved mandate: the Supreme Court accepted the graded response plan on 2 December 2016 in M.C. Mehta v. UOI (WP 13029/1985) and directed that it be notified. CAQM has implemented it across NCR since 2021. That writ petition was disposed of on 12 March 2026, and air pollution in the National Capital Region is now heard in a fresh suo motu case.

The 50% office-strength measure moved from Stage IV to Stage III on 21 Nov 2025 (CAQM order of 13 Dec 2025; PIB, 22 Nov 2025)
Worker compensation (Nov 2024): SC directed NCR states to use labour cess to provide weekly subsistence allowance during GRAP III/IV, and its order of 25 November 2024 says economic assistance should reach affected construction workers once Stage IV restrictions are imposed. In March 2025 The Tribune reported that states must compensate these workers even without a court order, and that Delhi told the Court it had paid 93,272 workers and was verifying the rest of those registered (The Tribune, 1 March 2025). On 17 December 2025 Delhi's Labour Minister announced Rs 10,000 for each registered and verified construction worker for the Stage III spell, with Stage IV days to be counted after revocation (ANI, 18 December 2025). Workers who are not registered are outside these payments.

Vehicle age restrictions: legal basis

10/15 Year Ban Rule

  • NGT (2014-15): Diesel >10 years, petrol >15 years banned
  • SC (Oct 2018): Confirmed; directed impounding
  • SC (12 Aug 2025): No coercive action against owners of 10-year-old diesel and 15-year-old petrol vehicles in Delhi-NCR while the Delhi government's review is heard (LiveLaw). A later order is reported to confine that protection to BS-IV and newer vehicles (SCC Online, Mar 2026); check the order text.
  • Enforcement: The review is pending; do not assume impounding is active for every vehicle.

Restricting polluting vehicles: the legal test

A restriction on trade in polluting vehicles has to pass the reasonableness test in Article 19(6), and the Supreme Court has read Article 21 to include the right to clean air (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991). Transport is about 39% of Delhi’s PM2.5 emissions inventory (TERI–ARAI 2018), but its modelled share of winter PM2.5 concentration is 28%, below industry’s 30%.

Your legal rights at a glance

Five pillars of law that protect your right to breathe clean air. Know them and cite them in your complaints.

Article 21, Constitution of India

Right to life includes the right to clean air. Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar, (1991) 1 SCC 598.

Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

Established CPCB and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). Mandates them to prevent, control, and abate air pollution. Section 31A: binding closure/direction orders.

Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

Umbrella legislation. Central government can take measures for environmental protection, set emission standards, and order closure of polluting units. Sections 3 & 5.

National Green Tribunal Act, 2010

Created a dedicated tribunal for environmental disputes. Any person can file an application. Filing fee: ₹1,000 where no compensation is claimed; no fee for BPL applicants claiming compensation (NGT Rules 2011). Individuals can file in person.

Right to Information Act, 2005

Right to access government data on pollution monitoring, NCAP funds, enforcement actions. Free for BPL; ₹10 otherwise. 30-day response mandate. Use the RTI Assistant →

What can I do from home? Step-by-step citizen recourse

You do not need to be a lawyer, activist, or expert. Here are five concrete steps any citizen can take from home to demand clean air. Each step is progressively more powerful.

STEP 1

File an RTI: free, 30-day response guaranteed

The simplest and most powerful tool. Any citizen can ask for pollution data, details of how budgets were spent, or enforcement records.

Who to write to:
  • CPCB PIO: for national monitoring data, emission norms, station uptime
  • Your State PCB PIO: for local station data, industry compliance, NCAP city plans
  • CAQM PIO: for GRAP enforcement records (NCR only)
What to ask:
  • CAAQMS data-availability percentage for your city, last 12 months
  • NCAP funds received vs. spent by your city, FY 2023–2026
  • Number of industrial inspections and closure orders issued, last 1 year
  • GRAP enforcement: fines levied, vehicles impounded, sites shut (NCR)
STEP 2

File a Complaint with CPCB

Report specific violations, such as illegal burning, industrial emissions or construction dust, directly to CPCB online.

How to file:
  • In Delhi-NCR, lodge the complaint on the CPCB website (cpcb.gov.in) using its complaint form. Outside Delhi-NCR, write to your State Pollution Control Board
  • Include: exact location, type of violation, date & time, photos/videos if possible
  • In Delhi-NCR you can also file via the SAMEER app (CPCB guidelines for Delhi-NCR air pollution complaints)
  • Delhi-NCR: nodal agencies are expected to redress SAMEER complaints within 24 hours (CPCB guidelines)

Tip: File with both CPCB and your State PCB simultaneously. It doubles the accountability.

STEP 3

Write to Your MP / MLA

Elected representatives respond to constituent pressure. A well-written letter citing local data is surprisingly effective.

Template letter (copy and customise):
Respected Shri/Smt [Name], I am a resident of [your locality], in [your constituency]. I am writing to draw your urgent attention to the air pollution crisis in our area. According to CPCB data, our city's annual average PM2.5 is [X] µg/m³, which is [Y] times the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. This causes an estimated [Z] premature deaths annually in our district (Lancet Countdown 2025). I request you to: 1. Raise this issue in Parliament / State Assembly during the next session 2. Write to the Environment Minister seeking an NCAP review for our city 3. Ensure NCAP funds allocated to our city are fully spent (as of [date], only [%] was spent, according to [source: Lok Sabha reply, PIB release or your RTI reply]) I am happy to share the full data report from JanVayu (janvayu.in) which sources all numbers from CPCB, NCAP, and peer-reviewed studies. Yours faithfully, [Your Name], [Your Address]

Find your representative: Election Commission of India  |  Lok Sabha Members Directory

STEP 4

Approach the National Green Tribunal (NGT)

The NGT is a dedicated environmental court. Any individual can file an application, and no lawyer is needed for individual applications.

Key facts:
  • Filing fee: ₹1,000 where no compensation is claimed; none for BPL applicants claiming compensation
  • Jurisdiction: Environmental damage, non-compliance with pollution norms, failure to implement court orders
  • Benches: Principal Bench (Delhi) + 4 zonal benches (Pune, Bhopal, Chennai, Kolkata)
  • Timeline: NGT must endeavour to dispose of applications within 6 months (s.18(3), NGT Act 2010)
  • Filing: Check the filing procedure on the Tribunal's website, greentribunal.gov.in

Note: For complex cases or those involving multiple states/industries, engaging an environmental lawyer is recommended. Many NGOs offer pro-bono legal support.

STEP 5

File a PIL (Public Interest Litigation)

For systemic failures affecting a community. Filed in the High Court or Supreme Court. Any citizen can file, and no special locus standi is required.

How PIL works:
  • Who can file: Any citizen, on behalf of the public. No need to be personally affected.
  • Where: High Court of your state (Article 226) or Supreme Court (Article 32)
  • Requires: A lawyer, but many environmental lawyers work pro bono.
  • Cost: Court fees vary by state; some HCs waive fees for PILs. Court fee for a petition under Article 32 is ₹500 (Supreme Court Rules, 2013, Third Schedule, Part I, item 5), with no court fee on habeas corpus petitions or on Article 32 petitions arising out of criminal proceedings (Order XXXVIII, rule 2). Check the current Rules before filing.
  • Timeline: Can lead to interim orders within weeks if urgency is demonstrated
Landmark PILs that changed India's air:
  • MC Mehta v Union of India (WP 13029/1985, disposed of March 2026): Led to CNG mandate in Delhi, vehicle-age limits, GRAP framework
  • Vellore Citizens v Union of India (1996): Established Polluter Pays and Precautionary Principles
  • Subhash Kumar v State of Bihar (1991): Right to clean air = Right to life under Article 21
  • M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India (2024): Right against climate degradation as a fundamental right
You have more power than you think. An RTI application costs ₹10 and 15 minutes. A complaint to CPCB costs nothing. A letter to your MP costs a stamp. Start with Step 1 today. The law is on your side.
