Where your city's PM2.5 comes from

Cleaning the air means knowing what's dirtying it. This ring shows the share of fine-particle (PM2.5) pollution from each major source in a city — drawn from the most credible source-apportionment study we could find for that city. Pick a city to see its mix.

How to read this — and its limits

What "source apportionment" means. Scientists work out how much each type of source contributes to the fine particles (PM2.5) you breathe, using one of two methods:

  • Receptor models (like Chemical Mass Balance) chemically fingerprint the particles actually measured in the air — so they capture secondary particles that form in the atmosphere, which show up here under "Other".
  • Emission inventories add up what each source emits. They're good on primary particles but don't separate out secondary aerosol or long-range transport, so their "Other" is often near zero.

Why you can't cleanly compare cities. Studies differ by method, by season (winter peaks look very different from annual averages), and by year. The mix is indicative, not a precise league table — each city names its own study and basis above the ring. Where a study only reported winter and summer, we've said so.

The buckets. Transport = vehicles. Industry = manufacturing (and, in some studies, thermal power). Biomass & burning = household cooking fuel, waste and crop-residue burning. Dust & construction = road/soil dust and building sites. Power = thermal power where the study separates it. Other = secondary aerosol, transboundary transport, and miscellaneous.

Primary sources: CREA, UrbanEmissions.info (APnA), CSIR-NEERI, CSTEP, TERI, IIT Kanpur, and state pollution control boards under NCAP.
