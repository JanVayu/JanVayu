About JanVayu

JanVayu (जनवायु, “People’s Air”) is a non-partisan, citizen-led platform that documents India’s air quality crisis: the live data, the human toll, the policies and the public memory. It brings together real-time PM2.5 across 157 Indian cities, a boundary atlas from state to village, a live forecast and farm-fire tracker, health and economic-impact research, policy and accountability tracking, RTI tools, the Ask JanVayu AI assistant, and a multilingual wall of citizen testimony. Every figure is sourced and open. The site keeps a public record and was built for the #AQIForJanHit effort.

Our mission

JanVayu exists because clean air is a fundamental right. We measure the gap between what governments promise and what people breathe, using independent data, peer-reviewed research and RTI responses that anyone can check for themselves.

This platform is part of AirQuality for Janhit by MMSF Fellows, AIPC and the broader #AQIForJanHit campaign. We use real-time monitoring data, peer-reviewed research, RTI responses and independent analysis to hold India’s air quality to account.

Principles

Scientific accuracy

PM2.5 primary reporting over AQI. WHO 2021 guideline alignment. GEMM model for health estimates. All claims sourced and verified.

Environmental justice

Air pollution falls hardest on marginalized communities. Our analysis centers the burden on informal workers, women, Dalit communities, and the urban poor.

Independent verification

Government self-reporting is insufficient. We correlate promises with measurable outcomes using RTI, satellite data, and academic research.

What we are building next

Just shipped (v26.6.232): Every page on this site now uses one stylesheet and follows one theme control. Until this release only index.html loaded it. The other eighteen pages each carried a private copy of the same colour names, fixed to light, so a change reached one page, and the ones with a dark mode followed your operating system instead of the button in the bar. Choosing light here with a dark desktop left half the site dark. Nothing failed while that was true, which is why it lasted. Two faults found on the way were not cosmetic. The per-pollutant pages had been printing a random number as the live level for every pollutant except PM2.5, and six of our functions were reporting the US-EPA sub-index as a concentration, so Ask JanVayu could tell you the air was twice as bad as it was. Both are fixed and now checked automatically.

Weather-normalised trends, brought up to date

Our 44-city de-weathered trends stop at 2024, because the XKDR archive’s CPCB feed ends 1 September 2025. Moving to CREA’s published measurements would make them current. Testing on Delhi in 2024 shows the two agree to a median of 0.000 µg/m³ per station-day, but CREA filters outliers and is systematically lower, by up to 0.98 µg/m³ a year in Hyderabad, nearly twice that city’s annual trend. So we will rebuild the whole series on one source rather than splice two. See the working note.

Time-aware heat

Surface temperature taken as a seasonal median instead of from a single clear-sky scene, which cuts single-day noise. Update, 2 October 2026: this has since shipped as a national pre-monsoon mosaic.

Your ward, shareable

Per-ward share cards: "my ward vs the city" snapshots for the air, heat and green layers. Update, 2 October 2026: shipped on 11 June 2026; the separate ward panel was later folded into the main atlas.

v26.6.175–186, 8–17 September 2026
Air we measured rather than modelled, and the wind separated from the policy in 44 cities
• This is the first air layer on the site built from instruments. JanVayu’s air layers are built to reach where monitors do not. A satellite estimate gives every one of India’s villages a 2024 annual figure, a bias-corrected atmospheric model carries it to the current year, and a peer-reviewed reconstruction reaches back to 1980. The satellite product and the reconstruction are calibrated against ground monitors, and the model layer is corrected against the satellite product. Each is labelled for what it is. What the site did not hold was the instrument record itself, which is what lets you check them. The India Air Quality Database (XKDR Forum, CC BY 4.0) carries CPCB’s own hourly record: 196.5 million readings from 558 stations. For 2024 the satellite layer and the station readings agree at r = 0.834, with the satellite about 2 µg/m³ lower. That is the direction to expect, because a district average includes the fields around the city.
• “Was it policy, or was it the wind?” now has an answer for 44 cities, not one. In September the honest answer was that removing the weather effect was blocked on data access, and it no longer is. Across 2018–2024, 33 cities are improving once weather is removed and 11 are not. The steepest real falls are Meerut −14.6, Varanasi −14.2 and Lucknow −14.0 µg/m³ a year. Getting worse with nothing to blame on the weather: Chandigarh +3.1, Gwalior +2.4, Mumbai +0.8. What is left after weather is not proof that policy caused it, because emissions, fuel mix and construction all sit inside it.
• Nine peer-reviewed papers joined the Reading List, taking it from 33 to 42, each checked against Crossref first. Two arrived with DOIs that resolve to nothing and were held. One arrived with a real DOI attached to an entirely different paper, which a DOI check passes and only reading the abstract catches.
• We had to correct two numbers about ourselves. The homepage advertised 29 peer-reviewed studies while the list held 33, and had been stale since July. And we recorded the XKDR archive as running to March 2026, which is what it reports and is badly misleading: CPCB’s feed into it stops on 1 September 2025, and the months after are two US Embassy monitors. A maximum date is not a coverage claim.
• Three blog posts were live and missing from the blog’s own sidebar, two of them for three days. Publishing a post updated the homepage list, and nothing forced anyone to touch the sidebar. The build now fails when the two disagree.
