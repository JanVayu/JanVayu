# Delhi must release its birth records, so we can find out what its air is doing to pregnancies

**Published:** 4 November 2026 | **Author:** Team JanVayu | **Reading time:** 6 min

---

On 22 May 2026 Newslaundry published Astha Savyasachi and Akankhya Rout's investigation, "5 years, over 21% rise in preterm birth rate: Delhi's air is cutting pregnancies short". Using month-wise birth data obtained under the Right to Information Act from Delhi's Directorate of Family Welfare, they report that recorded preterm births rose from 21,134 in 2021 to 31,963 in 2025, an increase of 51%, and that the preterm share of live births rose from 9.8% to 11.9%. Preterm births peak every August or September, which means the babies were in early pregnancy in the worst months of winter. A card summarising the report has since circulated on Instagram.

We think the report is right to treat this as an alarm. The evidence that PM2.5 harms pregnancy is already strong enough to act on. What Delhi lacks is the data to say how much of its own preterm rise is the air, and the Delhi government holds that data. It should release it. We have not seen the RTI figures, so every Delhi number here is as Newslaundry reports it.

## The evidence already points one way

A 2021 meta-regression in PLOS Medicine (Ghosh and colleagues) pooled studies and found that each 10 µg/m³ of ambient PM2.5 went with a 12% higher risk of preterm birth (risk ratio 1.12, 95% interval 1.06 to 1.19), an 11% higher risk of low birth weight, and a birth weight about 22 grams lower. Most of those studies came from North America, Europe and Australia, and the authors note that a few recent ones came from China, India, sub-Saharan Africa and South America.

India has studies of its own, and most of them link national surveys to satellite estimates of PM2.5.

- Xue and colleagues (Lancet Planetary Health, 2021) studied 34,197 mothers who had a pregnancy loss, 76.9% of them in India, using Demographic and Health Survey data from 1998 to 2016. Each 10 µg/m³ of PM2.5 went with an odds ratio of 1.03 (95% interval 1.02 to 1.05) for pregnancy loss, and they estimate about 349,681 losses a year in South Asia are attributable to PM2.5 above India's annual standard of 40 µg/m³, 7.1% of the region's total.
- deSouza and colleagues (Science of the Total Environment, 2022) linked 259,627 children in the 2015-16 survey to PM2.5 in the month of birth. Third-trimester PM2.5 went with higher neonatal mortality, an odds ratio of 1.016 (95% interval 1.003 to 1.030) per 10 µg/m³.
- Jana and colleagues (PLOS Global Public Health, 2025) used the 2019-21 survey and found higher odds of low birth weight (adjusted odds ratio 1.4) and preterm birth (1.7) with higher PM2.5, and a 12% higher prevalence of preterm birth per 10 µg/m³.
- Misra and Kulshreshtha (Journal of Environmental Economics and Management, 2026) used wind direction as an instrument and found that one standard deviation less in-utero PM2.5 raises average birth weight by about 1.1%.
- Parikh and colleagues (Reproductive Toxicology, 2026) followed 406 pregnant women in a hospital cohort. Each 10 µg/m³ of PM2.5 had a significant association with pre-eclampsia, preterm birth and low birth weight, and with cord-blood inflammatory markers, and black carbon was found on the foetal side of the placenta in the exposed group only.

The report says India has no study comparable to China's multi-city research. We did not check the Chinese studies. India's evidence is thinner than it should be, and the hospital cohort's authors write that the topic "remains largely unexplored" in India, but it is not absent.

The burden is large. The Lancet's 2023 estimates (Ohuma and colleagues) put global preterm births in 2020 at 13.4 million, with India having the most at 3.02 million, which is 22.6% of the world's total. The Lancet paper itself says "over 20%". The "more than 23%" quoted in the report and on the card comes from a 2025 review of the same figures, and 22.6% is what they give.

## What the Delhi count cannot yet say

It carries no exposure data. The RTI figures are counts of births by month, with no mother's exposure attached, so they show a peak in August and September and cannot show how much of it is the air.

The denominator also moved. If 21,134 preterm births were 9.8% of live births in 2021 and 31,963 were 11.9% in 2025, the implied number of recorded live births rose from about 216,000 to about 269,000, an increase of roughly a quarter in four years. That is our arithmetic from rounded rates, so the range is about 23% to 26%, and the report does not explain it. A change in which facilities report, or in how gestational age is recorded, could move the count without any change in biology.

Heat competes for the August peak. A baby born at 34 to 36 weeks in August spent the last trimester in the Delhi summer. A 2020 BMJ systematic review (Chersich and colleagues, 70 studies in 27 countries) found that the odds of preterm birth rose 1.05-fold per 1 °C increase in temperature and 1.16-fold during heatwaves. Heat and pollution can act together, and the August peak cannot separate them.

The doctors Newslaundry quotes see pregnancies for which they find no other explanation, and that clinical signal is worth taking seriously. One of them, Dr Archana Dhawan Bajaj, says air pollution "cannot be identified as the sole cause" and that the evidence to settle it "does not exist yet". We agree, and that is the reason for the ask below. Two further claims in the report, about folic acid absorption and a 1,400-pregnancy Varanasi study, we have not verified and do not repeat.

## What Delhi should do

Release facility-level birth records, with dated gestational age and the mother's area of residence, with identifying details removed. Paired with daily CPCB readings, they would let researchers compare mothers in the same hospital and the same season by their exposure, and test heat and pollution separately. Newslaundry says it sent questionnaires to the Delhi health department and the Union environment and health ministries and would update the report if they replied. We ask the Delhi government to answer in public, and we will add any study built on the records to the Reading List.
