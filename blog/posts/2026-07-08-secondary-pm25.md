# The Pollution You Can't See Being Emitted: Up to 42% of India's PM2.5 Is Made in the Sky

**Published:** 8 July 2026 | **Author:** Team JanVayu | **Reading time:** 4 min

---

When we picture air pollution, we picture a source: a truck's exhaust, a brick kiln's chimney, a field of burning stubble, a cloud of construction dust. Something is emitted, and we breathe it. But a large share of the fine particulate matter that shortens Indian lives was never emitted as particles at all. It is manufactured in the atmosphere, hours and hundreds of kilometres downwind, out of invisible gases. A 2026 analysis by the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA) puts a number on it: **up to 42% of India's PM2.5 burden is secondary** — chemically formed in the air rather than directly released.

This is not a footnote. It reshapes what "cleaning the air" has to mean.

## Primary vs secondary particles

**Primary** PM2.5 is emitted as particles: soot from diesel, fly ash, road and construction dust. You can point a control at it — a filter, a cover, a water spray.

**Secondary** PM2.5 starts as a gas. Sulphur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and ammonia (NH₃) drift out of smokestacks, tailpipes and farms, react with sunlight and each other, and condense into fine particles — ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate. By the time they exist as PM2.5, the "source" is a plume of gas that left a chimney a long way away. Using NASA's MERRA-2 reanalysis for 2024, CREA finds this secondary fraction reaches **42%** of the national PM2.5 load.

## Coal, sulphur, and the missing scrubbers

The dominant precursor in India is SO₂, and India is the **world's largest SO₂ emitter**. The single biggest contributor is coal-fired power: at least **60% of national SO₂ emissions**. That SO₂ becomes ammonium sulphate, and the fingerprints are regional — CREA attributes **42% of Chhattisgarh's** and **41% of Odisha's** PM2.5 to ammonium sulphate, both heavy coal-power states. Even in Delhi, roughly **one-third** of PM2.5 is secondary ammonium sulphate, rising to **49% in the post-monsoon** window.

Here is the policy gap that follows: the cheapest, highest-leverage fix for SO₂ is flue-gas desulphurisation (FGD) — scrubbers on power-plant stacks. Yet roughly **78% of India's coal-fired capacity still operates without FGD**, under repeatedly extended compliance deadlines. We are, in effect, choosing to keep making a large fraction of our own particulate matter.

## Why this breaks "dust-first" air policy

Much of India's clean-air spending is visible-source, visible-result: paving roads, spraying water, sweeping dust, greening verges. Those measures are real and worth doing — but they act mostly on *primary* coarse particles. They do almost nothing about a sulphate particle formed in the sky from a power-plant gas. As CREA puts it, "without addressing secondary particulate matter, improvements in air quality are likely to remain limited and short-lived."

This is also why two honest monitors in the same airshed can tell different stories, and why a city can pave every road and still miss its PM2.5 target. If you only fight the pollution you can see being emitted, you leave up to two-fifths of the problem untouched.

## What it would take

Cutting secondary PM2.5 means regulating **gases**, not just dust:

- **FGD on coal plants** — enforce the mandate instead of extending it. This is the largest single lever on SO₂.
- **NOₓ controls** on power and transport, which feed ammonium nitrate.
- **Ammonia** from agriculture — the least-regulated precursor, and the one that turns SO₂ and NOₓ into particles.
- **Composition monitoring**, not just mass — measuring *what* the PM2.5 is made of, so control money follows chemistry rather than optics.

## The JanVayu takeaway

We've added CREA's 2026 secondary-PM2.5 analysis to the [Reading List](https://www.janvayu.in/#resources). It reinforces a theme that runs through this platform: the honest picture of India's air is rarely the intuitive one. A ward isn't one number. A city's PM2.5 isn't one source. And a great deal of what you breathe was never emitted as something you could see — it was assembled overhead, out of gases that left a chimney nobody photographed.

Clean-air policy that ignores that chemistry is treating the smoke and missing the fire.
