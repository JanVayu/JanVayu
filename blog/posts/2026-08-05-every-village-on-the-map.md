# Every Village in India Is Now on the Map — Including the Ones No One Measures

**Published:** 5 August 2026 | **Updated:** 6 August 2026 | **Author:** Team JanVayu | **Reading time:** 7 min

---

India's air pollution story is told almost entirely through its cities. Delhi in November. Mumbai's winter haze. The annual ranking that puts another Indian town at the top of a global list nobody wants to lead. But about two-thirds of the country doesn't live in a city (Census 2011) — and until now, if you went looking for your village on our map, you wouldn't have found it.

Today you will. We've added every one of India's **584,615 villages** to the live map, drawn as the actual administrative boundaries your panchayat and district records use. Open the [map](https://www.janvayu.in/#map), press **Villages** in the row of layer buttons, and zoom in. Your village is in there.

## What you'll see when you find it

Tap any village and a small card opens with its name, its district, its state — and two numbers that are deliberately kept apart.

The first is an **annual average**: what this village breathed over the course of a year, estimated from satellite at about one kilometre. Every single village has one. That figure is what the rest of this post is about.

The second is **right now** — and here the card often has to admit defeat. If there's a monitor reasonably nearby you'll get a live estimate, with a note saying which monitor it came from and how far away it is. If there isn't, the card says something blunter: **no monitor close enough for a live estimate.**

Most villages will show that second message. It isn't a bug we're planning to fix. It's the most honest thing the live map can say, and it's why the annual figure matters.

*Update, 6 August: this post originally went out when only the live estimate existed, and most villages showed nothing at all. The annual satellite layer shipped the next day, so every village now has a number. The paragraphs below have been updated; the argument hasn't changed.*

## The gap you can now see

India has roughly **565 continuous air quality monitoring stations** (CPCB, via CREA's *Tracing the Hazy Air*, January 2026). Set that against 584,615 villages and the arithmetic does the arguing for you. The monitoring network was built where the attention is, which means it was built in cities.

For years the honest answer to "how bad is the air in my village?" has been "nobody is measuring." That answer is easy to say and easy to forget. It's much harder to forget when you're looking at a screen full of village outlines and the map keeps telling you, one tap at a time, that it doesn't know.

So we've made the silence visible. Zoom across rural Bihar or interior Karnataka and tap a few villages: the "right now" line keeps coming back empty. That is a picture of who India's live air monitoring actually covers, and who it doesn't.

## What the satellite can tell us, and what it can't

Satellites can't tell you today's air in your village — nobody can, honestly, and we won't pretend otherwise. What they can do is give you the **yearly average**, and they can do it everywhere, because they aren't waiting for a monitor to exist.

So every village now carries an annual PM2.5 figure derived from satellite measurements at about one kilometre, calibrated against ground monitors. Across all 584,615 villages, three things stand out:

- **Not one meets the WHO annual guideline of 5 µg/m³.** Not a single village in India.
- **371,938 of them — 63.6% — exceed India's own annual limit of 40.**
- The median village sits at **43.7 µg/m³**, roughly nine times the WHO guideline.

The cleanest places are the island districts — Andaman & Nicobar and Lakshadweep, around 12–20 µg/m³ — and parts of Kerala. The dirtiest are the districts of Delhi, at 94–98.

Two honest limits. This is a yearly average, so it says nothing about a bad week in November. And a one-kilometre estimate smooths out very local sources: a brick kiln or a smelter at the edge of your village won't appear in it. Byrnihat, the small industrial town that topped IQAir's global city ranking, reads far lower on this layer than its own ground monitor does. The satellite is good at the air a whole district breathes, and blind to the chimney next door.

## Rural air is not clean air

It would be convenient if the gap didn't matter — if the unmeasured places were simply the clean ones. They aren't.

The clearest example is what happens indoors. Around **70% of rural Indian women still cook with solid fuels** — wood, dung cake, crop residue (NFHS-5, 2019-21). They spend three to five hours a day near the chulha, breathing PM2.5 at 20–40 times the WHO annual guideline. That happens in their own kitchens, every day, and no outdoor monitor would catch it even if one were nearby. It's a burden carried mostly by women and by the small children who sit beside them.

This is also why the death toll has two honest numbers rather than one, and we think both are worth knowing. Ambient outdoor PM2.5 is linked to about **1.72 million deaths a year in India** (Lancet Countdown 2025, using 2022 data). Count household air pollution as well and the figure rises to roughly **2.0 million** (State of Global Air 2025, using 2023 data). Different scopes, different methods, both real. The second one is where a lot of rural India lives.

Then there's the pollution that doesn't respect the city limit sign. Stubble smoke drifts across whole states. Brick kilns, small industry and highway traffic sit in places with no station for a hundred kilometres. A village can be well outside a city and still be inside the same airshed.

## What this layer is, and what it isn't

We want to be precise about this, because it would be easy to oversell.

The colours on the map are the **annual satellite figure** — a real per-village estimate. What we have deliberately *not* done is colour villages by today's air. With 565 monitors and 584,615 villages, spreading those live readings across the country and painting each village a confident shade of orange would look authoritative and mean almost nothing. That would be inventing precision we don't have.

So the live number stays out of the colours and sits on its own line in the card. When it appears at all, it's because there's a monitor within about 50 kilometres, and the card names that monitor and its distance so you can judge how much to trust it. Air measured in one place is not air measured in yours, and we say so.

The outlines themselves are administrative geography, not measurement — the official boundaries and nothing more.

The boundaries themselves come from the Local Government Directory via [indianopenmaps.com](https://indianopenmaps.com), the community-run mirror of India's public geodata that also supplies our ward, constituency and district layers. They reflect records as of 2023, so a few very recent reorganisations may not have caught up yet.

## Why we bothered

There's a version of clean air advocacy that only ever talks about the capital in winter. It gets attention, and attention matters. But it quietly implies that the problem has an address, and that most of the country is fine.

Putting 584,615 villages on a map is our small argument against that. We can now tell you roughly what each one breathes over a year — and the answer is that essentially none of them are fine. What we still can't tell you is what your village is breathing this morning, and that gap is a choice someone made about where to put the monitors. The country deserves a map that shows where it lives, not just where it's measured.
